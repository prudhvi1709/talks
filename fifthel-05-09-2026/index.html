Is the cheaper model good enough. I've had to answer that exact question three times this year, and I got it wrong on the first pass every single time. Not because the model was bad. Because I was reading the wrong number.

Three different systems, nothing in common between them except that question. An OCR engine, for a document pipeline. An embedding model, for retrieval. A vision verifier, for grading CCTV compliance. I'll go through them one at a time, in order, and show you the number that fooled me in each.

First case. This was a document pipeline - scanned government pages get OCR'd into a corpus, and every answer downstream has to trace back to the exact question it came from. So: Tesseract, a free, local OCR engine. Someone challenged me on a benchmark row - Tesseract was scoring 90.03 word accuracy on Hindi, against hosted frontier OCR models at 92 to 95. I re-ran it. On word accuracy this looked like a coin flip - about two points separated ten engines across four providers. On the number that mattered, it wasn't close, on either subset.

Third case, a different system entirely. This started as a manual process - someone watching long, unedited CCTV recordings by hand to check compliance - and we proposed automating it with AI. That's not the client's footage, by the way - I can't show that - it's a public dataset, same kind of overhead coverage, different facility. A frontier model reads the footage and grades compliance against the client's own rulebook - and that grade drives a real decision, so a wrong one has consequences. There was also a hard constraint going in: the client didn't want raw CCTV footage leaving their infrastructure to any hosted model, so whatever checked the frontier model's citations had to run locally too. That's why SAM 3.1, self-hosted on one workstation GPU, cross-checking every frame it cited before anything reached a person. Frontier proposes, small model verifies. It's a good pattern. I still believe in it.

Agreeing with the frontier model isn't the same question as being right.

I measured it three ways. It disagreed with the frontier model on 38 of 65 scored checks - and most of those disagreements turned out to be its own prompt bugs, not real disagreements. That's the trap: agreement-with-the-frontier-model looks like a benchmark, but it isn't the metric that matters. Costs nothing to check - the scans kept every detection and score, so I re-ran the sweep offline. At 0.25, session A lands within 30 seconds of ground truth. Session B at 0.25 is 25 minutes early; raise the threshold to 0.35, it swings to 13 minutes early; raise it to 0.40, 70 minutes late - nothing usable in between. Session C never scores above 0.226 in the entire recording - above that, it detects nothing, ever. Peak scores range from 0.226 to 0.586 for the exact same prompt, same object class. The scores aren't comparable across sessions, so a global threshold isn't a coherent idea here. And here's the actual defect, not just the symptom: the prompt said "conveyor" - it only had to see the belt itself. The compliance rule needed the conveyor's full extent, including where it runs into the trailer, because that's where the violation happens. Demoted. I tried every operating point in the sweep. Best case: one session out of three, correct. Excluding its output instead: two out of three, correct. That's what took it out of the verdict - it stopped being trusted alone, but it kept running. And this wasn't a tuning problem - no confidence cutoff fixes a detector that's answering the wrong question. The benchmark instinct here was agreement with the frontier model. The metric that actually mattered was correctness against independent ground truth - and once the prompt itself was pointed at the wrong region, no amount of threshold tuning was ever going to close that gap. What was missing wasn't a better threshold, it was judgment: something that could read the same evidence and reason about it, not just score it against a fixed number.

So why an adjudicator, and why not from the start? An adjudicator here just means a checker, not a detector - it doesn't look at the footage itself, it looks at what the other two already said and decides whether that holds up. Why introduce it now: the frontier model alone couldn't be trusted, and SAM alone turned out to be a fixed-threshold detector with no way to reason about context - tuning it couldn't fix that. What was actually missing was judgment, applied after both cheaper layers had already run and shown their limits. Here's the part that actually made it work, and it's not the model - it's how it was run. This wasn't a single API call fed the whole clip. It was Claude Code, a coding agent, put in a sandbox with the frames themselves. That meant it could crop into a corner of the image, flip it, zoom in, rerun the same frame from a different angle - actually go look, the way a person reviewing footage would, instead of committing to one answer from one fixed view. That's the entire reason it beat a plain API call: not a bigger model, a model with hands. 132 calls, about 17 seconds each, about a dollar fifty for the session - cheap, given what it replaced. Its contract stayed disciplined throughout: allowed to downgrade severity, never to upgrade it.

Its canonical win, written up the same evening, was catching a hallucinated conveyor timestamp of 00:25:00. Except 00:25:00 was correct - confirmed by SME ground truth, recorded independently. And the verifier had a rule stating timestamp claims must be returned unverifiable unless a clock is burnt into the frame, using this exact timestamp as its own worked example. The verdict was fixed before the model saw a pixel, and so was the sentence announcing it.

A rule-produced category
got read as a finding.

"Could not be confirmed" - read as "caught."

A three-valued verifier had been collapsed to two values by the humans reading it, and "could not confirm" got filed as "caught." A category produced by rule rather than evidence is a constant. And a constant read as a signal is the purest way to measure nothing while feeling well-instrumented.

Not the same mistake

Three different failures.

OCR: wrong metric Embeddings: wrong slice Verifier: faulty evaluator

One thing in common: the evaluation signal didn't represent the production question.

Every public number here was directional, not decisive - what caught each failure was a check I built for that task, not a bigger benchmark.

These are three different failures, not the same mistake - OCR was the wrong metric, embeddings was the wrong evaluation slice, the verifier was a faulty evaluator. What's actually common across all three: the evaluation signal didn't represent the production question. That's the thing to check for, before you trust any number. And notice what all three fixes have in common: none of them was a better public benchmark. Word accuracy, cosine similarity on a standard eval set, agreement-with-a-frontier-model - all directionally useful, none of them decisive. What caught the failure, every time, was a small, task-specific check I built myself. That's the actual argument for having your own internal benchmark: not instead of public ones, but because a public benchmark tells you the model is in the right neighborhood, and only your own check tells you if it does the one thing your product needs.

One honest complication, so this doesn't collapse into "just pick the right metric." In the OCR case, the entire ten-engine field - all four providers - spanned about two points of word accuracy. One correction to a font table improved Hindi misread three to four times, on all ten engines at once. If the question was whether a smaller or cheaper model was good enough, that comparison is the answer, and it was never about the model - it was a data-layer fix that dwarfed every model difference in the field. And the counterweight, so I'm not overclaiming rigor I didn't have: every Hindi score I'd published before that correction was measured against a corrupted answer key. The numbers were wrong. The rank order of all ten engines, and every decision taken on it, wasn't.

Takeaways

Picking the number before you benchmark is the baseline. Two things past that:

1. The model was rarely the lever.

~2 points across ten engines · one font-table fix moved it 3-4x.

2. The verifier's cleanest win can be its own rule.

"Unverifiable by policy" filed as "caught a hallucination."

Picking the number before you benchmark is the baseline - you already know that one. Two things past it. One: the model was rarely the actual lever. Ten OCR engines spanned about two points of accuracy - one font-table fix moved Hindi misread three to four times, on all ten engines, at once. Half of this talk was a model comparison. Check whether the leverage is even in the model before you spend that effort on the comparison. Two: a verifier's most confident-looking catch can be its own rule firing, not a finding - "unverifiable by policy" got filed by a person as "caught a hallucination," on a timestamp that was actually correct. If you're stacking a verifier on a verifier - which is exactly what an agent harness is - the cleanest, most reassuring-looking catch is the one most worth doubting.
